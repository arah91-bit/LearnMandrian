# Tone-ear spike results — 2026-07-02

**Question:** can a local DSP pipeline reliably hear Mandarin tones from a voice
it has never heard, well enough to power a pronunciation tutor?
**Answer: yes — and it embarrasses the audio-LLM approach commercial apps use.**

## Setup

- 128 isolated Mandarin syllables (8 minimal quadruplets × 4 tones × 4 OpenAI
  TTS voices), generated by `gen_syllables.py` into `audio/` (gitignored,
  regenerable).
- Pipeline (`tone_ear.py`): two-pass adaptive Praat pitch tracking (Hirst
  method) → longest voiced run with creak-gap interpolation → semitones re
  clip median → 24-point resampled contour + duration → classifier.
- All evaluation is **leave-one-voice-out**: the test voice is never in
  training, simulating the learner's unseen voice.

## Results

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Tone ID, unseen voice (logreg) | **83.3%** pooled (chance 25%) |
| Tone ID, unseen voice (nearest template) | 80.8% |
| gpt-audio-1.5 asked to name the tone | **40.4%** of usable answers; 25% of replies unparseable |
| Planted wrong tone detected exactly (`plant_errors.py`) | **67.5%** (335 WORLD-resynthesis transplants) |
| Planted wrong tone flagged as *something* wrong | **89.0%** |

Confusions concentrate where real human learners and listeners also struggle
(tone 2 vs 3; low-register male voice hardest). Tone 1 and 4 are near-perfect.

## Conclusions

1. **The ear-vs-brain split is validated.** A ~100-line local pipeline beats a
   frontier audio LLM at tone perception by 2× — this is why "talk to the AI
   tutor" apps can't correct tones. Conversation goes STT→LLM; tone judgment
   must come from the DSP path and be fed to the LLM as structured data.
2. **Cross-speaker generalization works** with speaker-relative semitone
   normalization — no enrollment/calibration needed for a new voice.
3. **Known limits of this spike:** TTS-generated ground truth (imperfect but
   demonstrably mostly right — random labels couldn't yield 83% LOVO), isolated
   syllables only, linear classifier, n=120.

## Next steps

- Retrain + properly evaluate on **Tone Perfect** (MSU; 9,864 human clips,
  6 speakers — requested 2026-07-02, pending approval). Literature reports
  95%+ with small CNNs on this data.
- Add contour-shape features or a small MLP; fix weak transplant pairs
  (T4→T2, T1→T2).
- Extend to syllables-in-context (tone sandhi, neutral tone) via forced
  alignment for syllable boundaries.
- Then: app scaffolding (voice orb / auth / deploy patterns from LifeLog),
  `/srv/docker/languagetutor` + test instance.

# Retrain on Tone Perfect — 2026-07-07

Access to the MSU Tone Perfect corpus came through: 9,837 usable clips (of
9,840; 3 unvoiced) across 6 real speakers (FV1-3, MV1-3), 410 syllables ×
tones 1-4, extracted via `tone_perfect_eval.py`. Evaluation is
**leave-one-speaker-out** — the direct analogue of the TTS spike's LOVO, now
on real voices instead of synthetic ones.

## Results

| Model | Pooled LOSO accuracy |
|---|---|
| Logistic regression (the spike's classifier, unchanged) | **74.1%** |
| Random forest (300 trees) | **92.8%** |
| Gradient boosting | 92.4% |
| MLP (32,16) | 91.4% |

Logistic regression *regressed* on real speech relative to its 83.3% on TTS
voices — real tone contours, especially tone 3's creaky dip, aren't linearly
separable the way clean synthetic templates are. Nonlinear models close
almost all of the gap to the ~95% CNN benchmarks in the literature. Random
forest was picked for production: best accuracy, no feature scaling, no
convergence tuning, and refits from raw features at app-import time in ~1-2s
(300 trees, n_jobs=-1) — same "retrain at import" design as before.

Confusion (random forest, rows=true, cols=predicted):

```
  T1: [2380, 0,    78,   1]
  T2: [0,    2284, 174,  2]
  T3: [53,   97,   2132, 178]
  T4: [0,    0,    127,  2331]
```

Tone 3 went from 35.5% correct (logreg) to 86.7% (random forest). Remaining
confusion is exactly the classic pairs — T2↔T3 and T3↔T4 — the same ones
human listeners and learners struggle with, not pipeline artifacts (the
per-tone mean contours are phonetically correct dip/rise/fall shapes at both
classifiers; only the *linear separability* of individual instances differed).

**Shipped:** `tone_train.json` regenerated from all 9,837 Tone Perfect clips
(replacing the 120 TTS-voice samples); `tone_ear.py` switched from
`LogisticRegression` to `RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300)`. The raw
corpus and per-clip feature cache stay out of git (gitignored, regenerable
from the Tone Perfect mp3s per speaker/syllable naming convention).

## Next steps

- Tone 2/3 boundary is still the weak point — consider adding a creak/HNR
  feature so the classifier can tell "real dip" from "lost pitch track."
- Re-run `plant_errors.py` and the audio-LLM baseline against the new
  classifier for an apples-to-apples update of the whole results table.
- Extend to syllables-in-context (tone sandhi, neutral tone) — Tone Perfect
  is isolated-syllable only, same limitation as before.
